\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Speech deepfake detection has evolved rapidly alongside neural text-to-speech
synthesis. Early vocoders such as WaveNet~\cite{wavenet} and HiFi-GAN~\cite{hifigan}
produced waveforms imperceptible from natural speech, and subsequent adversarial
post-filters~\cite{malafide,malacopula} further obscure synthesis artefacts. The
research community has responded with increasingly powerful countermeasures, most
notably those built on large self-supervised speech representations such as
wav2vec~2.0~\cite{wav2vec2}, HuBERT~\cite{hubert}, WavLM~\cite{wavlm}, and
XLS-R~\cite{xlsr}, which transfer acoustic knowledge from hundreds of thousands
of hours of unlabelled speech. Evaluations are structured around the ASVspoof
challenge series~\cite{asvspoof2019,asvspoof2021,liu2023asvspoof,asvspoof5}.

Fusion of several countermeasures is the standard way to the top of such
leaderboards. The winning ASVspoof~5 open-condition system fuses twelve single
systems across three back-end families~\cite{ustc2024}. This paper asks a
narrower question: \textit{what must fusion members differ in for fusion to pay?}
We build four detectors that share a training corpus, a frozen self-supervised
backbone, an adaptation scheme, an augmentation policy, a seed and a schedule,
and differ only in their back-end---time-mean pooling, attentive
pooling~\cite{attentive_pool}, a cepstral branch, and graph
attention~\cite{gat_spoof}---plus a fifth varying the backbone itself. We then
measure not just whether they fuse, but \textit{how many independent systems they
amount to}.

The answer is uncomfortable. Across three architectural axes and two backbones,
the four members behave as \textbf{1.10 effective systems out of four}. The first
principal component explains 95.5\% of the score variance and loads within 0.006 of
$0.50$ on every member: they are, in information terms, one system wearing four
hats. The only intervention that moved the number was a change of
\textit{view}---opening the time axis that time-mean pooling discards---and the
0.10~\eer{} points it bought came from 3.2\% of the variance.

We report three further findings, each of which complicates the leaderboard
reading of this task.

\begin{enumerate}\itemsep2pt
\item \textbf{The development split cannot select these systems.}
  ASVspoof~5's train and development partitions are 100\% uncoded while 75\% of
  the evaluation set is coded. Over all 15 member subsets, the rank correlation
  between development and evaluation \eer{} is $\rho{=}+0.011$ ($p{=}0.97$).
  Adding a neural-codec development view raises it only to $+0.236$ ($p{=}0.40$).

\item \textbf{In-domain rank inverts out of domain.}
  On an in-the-wild evaluation set~\cite{intothewild}, our best in-domain member
  is the worst, and our third-best is the best by a wide margin. All systems
  degrade by $2.5$--$3.8\times$.

\item \textbf{The evaluation set is smaller than it looks.}
  Its 680{,}774 clips derive from 171{,}602 source utterances. Bootstrap
  intervals that resample clips are about $2\times$ too narrow.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Self-Supervised Front Ends for Anti-Spoofing}

Large-scale self-supervised pre-training has transformed anti-spoofing
countermeasures. wav2vec~2.0~\cite{wav2vec2} and HuBERT~\cite{hubert} learn
acoustic representations from unlabelled data via contrastive and
masked-prediction objectives; WavLM~\cite{wavlm} extends this to full-stack
speech tasks; XLS-R~\cite{xlsr} scales to cross-lingual data.
Wang and Yamagishi~\cite{wang2021ssl} systematically study these front ends on
ASVspoof~2019 and~2021. Tak et al.~\cite{tak2022wav2vec} show that fine-tuning
wav2vec~2.0 with augmentation sets state-of-the-art \eer{} on both tasks. Our
work uses WavLM-Large adapted with low-rank adaptation~\cite{lora} (3.15M
trainable parameters).

\subsection{Back-End Design and Pooling}

Attentive statistics pooling~\cite{attentive_pool} learns frame weights from a
context vector. Multi-head factorised attentive pooling (MHFA)~\cite{mhfa}
factorises comparison across multiple phonetic clusters. RawNet2~\cite{rawnet2}
operates end-to-end from the waveform using Sinc filters and gated recurrent
units. Graph attention networks for anti-spoofing~\cite{gat_spoof} model spectral
sub-band relationships; AASIST~\cite{aasist} extends this with heterogeneous
spectro-temporal cross-attention.

\subsection{Data Augmentation and Neural Codecs}

RawBoost~\cite{rawboost} adds impulsive, coloured, or linear predictive noise at
the waveform level. A key extension in this work is applying
EnCodec~\cite{encodec} on the GPU during training to simulate evaluation
conditions C04 and C07, which a CPU-bound DataLoader cannot handle.

\subsection{Generative Attacks and Adversarial Filters}

The speech spoofs in ASVspoof~5 are generated by neural text-to-speech and voice
conversion systems. WaveNet~\cite{wavenet} and HiFi-GAN~\cite{hifigan} represent
the autoregressive and GAN-based vocoder families. Adversarial
post-filters---Malafide~\cite{malafide} and Malacopula~\cite{malacopula}---apply
learned perturbations designed to evade specific detectors.

\subsection{Evaluation Datasets}

The ASVspoof series has been the dominant anti-spoofing benchmark since
2015~\cite{asvspoof2019,asvspoof2021,liu2023asvspoof,asvspoof5}.
WaveFake~\cite{wavefake} provides a complementary dataset generated by six neural
vocoders. M\"{u}ller et al.~\cite{intothewild} collected the In-the-Wild dataset
showing lab-trained detectors suffer substantial degradation on found audio.

\subsection{Metrics and Comparison Systems}

The tandem detection cost function~\cite{tdcf} assesses countermeasures jointly
with an ASV back-end. ASVspoof~5~\cite{asvspoof5} uses a simplified variant with
spoof prior 0.05. Our main comparison points are USTC-KXDIGIT~\cite{ustc2024}
at 2.59\% \eer{} / 0.0750 \mindcf{} (twelve fused systems) and
SZU-AFS~\cite{szuafs} at 4.04\% \eer{} / 0.115 \mindcf{} (single system), both
verified from published arXiv reports.

Table~\ref{tab:data} and Fig.~\ref{fig:dataset} describe the corpora. Three
properties of ASVspoof~5~\cite{asvspoof5} drive everything that follows.

\textbf{Disjoint attacks.} A01--A08, A09--A16 and A17--A32 do not overlap, so a
system is always tested on spoofing algorithms it has never seen.

\textbf{Channel mismatch between selection and test.} Train and development are
uncoded; the evaluation set is 75\% coded with eleven conditions. We simulate ten
of eleven during training; only C11 (device variation) is never simulated.
Section~\ref{sec:selection} shows this mismatch breaks model selection.

\textbf{Source-utterance structure inflates the apparent sample size.}
The 680{,}774 evaluation clips derive from \textbf{171{,}602 source utterances}
(mean 3.97 clips per source). Treating clips as independent in a bootstrap
understates every interval by roughly $\sqrt{4}$. All intervals in this paper use
a clustered bootstrap over source utterances.

\section{Systems}
\label{sec:systems}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/fig1_architecture}
\caption{All four members share the waveform pipeline, the augmentation policy,
and the adapted WavLM-Large backbone, differing only in the back-end that maps
per-layer frames to a score.}
\label{fig:arch}
\end{figure}

Fig.~\ref{fig:arch} shows the common pipeline. A waveform is augmented then
passed through WavLM-Large~\cite{wavlm} adapted with LoRA of rank~16 on the
attention projections~\cite{lora} (3.15M trainable parameters out of 315M total).
Training augmentation applies one of eight simulated codecs with probability 0.5
and, with probability 0.3, EnCodec~\cite{encodec} on the GPU.

\textit{Pool} (0.26M parameters). Takes the time-mean of each layer, learns a
softmax weighting over layers, and applies a two-layer MLP.

\textit{MHFA} (0.53M). Keeps frames rather than pooling first. Following
Peng et al.~\cite{mhfa}, it learns two separate layer weightings---one producing
keys, one values---and pools over time by multi-head attention.

\textit{LFCC} (0.50M). Adds a second representation: 20 linear-frequency
cepstral coefficients with first and second deltas, concatenated before attentive
pooling~\cite{attentive_pool}. Part of its evidence cannot correlate with anything
the backbone encodes.

\textit{Graph} (0.30M). Adds a second topology: graph attention over time slots
and backbone layers, reusing the graph attention layer of AASIST~\cite{aasist}.
This is \textit{not} AASIST---there is no heterogeneous cross-attention and no
master node.

Members are combined by the unweighted mean of scores standardised on the
development \texttt{calib} split.

\section{Experimental Protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}

Models are selected on the development \texttt{select} split (attacks
A09--A16), scale statistics are fit on the speaker-disjoint \texttt{calib} half,
and the evaluation set is scored once. Metrics are \eer{} and \mindcf{} under the
official ASVspoof~5 cost model (spoof prior $0.05$, $C_\text{miss}{=}1$,
$C_\text{fa}{=}10$)~\cite{asvspoof5,tdcf}, plus $\text{min}C_\text{llr}$.
Differences are tested with a paired bootstrap clustered on source utterance.

All members use seed 1337, batch size 32, eight epochs, head learning rate
$10^{-3}$ and backbone learning rate $10^{-5}$. \textbf{Limitation stated up
front:} every member is single-seed. Earlier three-seed experiments gave a spread
of 0.33~\eer{} points on frozen single models---larger than the 0.10-point fusion
gain we report.

\label{sec:rank}

\begin{figure*}[t]

We summarise a member set by the eigen-spectrum of its score correlation matrix,
reporting the participation ratio $(\sum_i \lambda_i)^2 / \sum_i \lambda_i^2$
and entropy rank $\exp(-\sum_i p_i \log p_i)$ with $p_i{=}\lambda_i/\sum_j \lambda_j$.

1.26), with PC1 explaining 95.5\% of the variance and loadings
$0.501, 0.496, 0.501, 0.502$. On the out-of-domain set the figure is 1.15 of 4.
Changing pooling, representation, topology \textit{and} backbone shifts the
effective rank by only 0.1.

\section{Adversarial Robustness}
\label{sec:robust}

\begin{table*}[t]
\centering

under an SNR-constrained $L_2$ budget~\cite{madry2018}. Realised SNR is reported.
Four routes are closed outright; waveform adversarial training is the only one

\label{tab:robust}
\small

We use PGD~\cite{madry2018} under an SNR-constrained $L_2$ budget; a
query-limited SPSA black-box attacker with 320 queries per clip; and white-box /
transfer variants. A gradient-bearing attacker succeeds on essentially every clip
at 30\,dB SNR against four of the five defences.

\textbf{Waveform adversarial training is the one route that moves.} Hardening the
path the attacker actually takes---half of each batch replaced by 30\,dB PGD
examples crafted through the backbone---drops white-box success from 1.000 to 0.953
at 30\,dB, and transfer success from 1.000 to 0.739. Its matched control, trained
without the adversarial half, sits at 1.000 on every row, confirming the effect is
the defence.

\textbf{The price is severe.} Clean \eer{} rises from 3.31\% to 7.09\%, a paired
difference of $+0.0378$ (95\% CI $[+0.0372,\,+0.0384]$ over 680{,}774 clips).
Codec conditions take the worst damage (C07 11.06\%, C08 10.08\%). Our contribution
here is a measurement rather than a method: under this threat model, SSL detectors
are not defensible at usable accuracy.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limit}

\textbf{Single seed.} Every system in Table~\ref{tab:members} is one seed.
Earlier three-seed experiments gave a 0.33~\eer{} point spread, larger than the
0.10-point fusion gain.

\textbf{One corpus.} We train on ASVspoof~5 alone. USTC-KXDIGIT~\cite{ustc2024}
trains on four corpora; we cannot separate a weaker architecture from narrower
supervision.

\textbf{Clustered intervals matter.} Clip-level bootstrap intervals on this corpus
are about $2\times$ too narrow. Our conclusions survive correction because
differences are many standard errors wide.

\textbf{One out-of-domain set.} In-the-Wild has its own biases: public figures,
internet audio, an inverted class prior. One set measures a gap; it does not
characterise generalisation.

% =========================================================================
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Four detectors differing in pooling, representation, topology, and backbone amount
to 1.10 independent systems. Architectural variety is not the same as error
diversity; only a change in what the model \textit{sees} moved the correlation.
The development split cannot rank these systems, costing 0.28~\eer{} points
against its own oracle; and in-domain ranking inverts out of domain, so the best
leaderboard member transfers worst.

Our selected system reaches \textbf{3.21\% \eer{} / 0.0905 \mindcf{}}, ahead of
SZU-AFS at 4.04\% / 0.115~\cite{szuafs} and short of the twelve-system winner
at 2.59\% / 0.0750~\cite{ustc2024}. The more useful contribution is the
measurement around that number: a reproducible way to quantify how many systems
an ensemble really contains, and evidence that the standard selection procedure on
this benchmark does not work.

Figures are generated by \texttt{paper/make\_figures.py} from a single JSON of
measured values, so no number in the text can disagree with a figure. Every
experiment, including failures and incorrect predictions, is recorded in

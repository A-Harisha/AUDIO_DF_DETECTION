\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

% Part A: Task and application.
Speech deepfake detection targets the binary decision of whether an utterance was
produced by a human or by a text-to-speech or voice-conversion system. It sits at
the intersection of audio forensics and automatic speaker verification (ASV):
detectors are deployed to protect ASV systems from being bypassed by synthesised
speech, and their failure has direct consequences in biometric authentication,
media integrity, and online trust. The ASVspoof challenge series~\cite{asvspoof2019,
asvspoof2021,liu2023asvspoof,asvspoof5} has structured the field, releasing corpora
with progressively stronger spoofing algorithms and defining the tandem detection
cost function~\cite{tdcf} as the primary evaluation metric.

% Part B: Challenge chain: SSL front ends → ensemble fusion → the gap we fill.
Neural speech synthesis has advanced to the point where individual vocoders such as
WaveNet~\cite{wavenet} and HiFi-GAN~\cite{hifigan} produce waveforms that human
listeners cannot reliably distinguish from natural speech. The research community
has responded with self-supervised pre-training front ends---wav2vec~2.0~\cite{wav2vec2},
HuBERT~\cite{hubert}, WavLM~\cite{wavlm}, XLS-R~\cite{xlsr}---that transfer
acoustic representations from unlabelled speech to the countermeasure task, and
these front ends have dramatically improved \eer{}. The current frontier is
ensemble fusion: the winning ASVspoof~5 system combines twelve single systems and
attributes most of its relative gain to diversity across those members~\cite{ustc2024}.

% Part B (continued): the unresolved challenge.
Despite this, the field has no operational way to measure whether members of a
fusion system are actually diverse. System selection is based entirely on held-out
accuracy, and ``diversity'' is inferred post-hoc from the fusion gain. This
creates a critical ambiguity: if twelve architecturally distinct systems fuse well,
it is impossible to tell whether they succeed because they genuinely disagree on
errors or because the fusion gain is driven by variance reduction in an overfit
score distribution. The question matters practically---building twelve systems is
expensive---and methodologically: if architectural variation does not produce error
independence, the field is assembling large ensembles without understanding why
they work.

% Part C: Our insight and pipeline.
The central insight of this work is that \emph{error correlation tracks what a
model sees, not how it is parameterised}. A system that time-averages backbone
features and a system with graph attention over those same features produce nearly
identical per-utterance scores, because both observe the same averaged
representation; a system that keeps the time axis open and learns frame-level
attention sees structurally different evidence. We operationalise this insight
by defining two scalar independence metrics---participation ratio ($\PR$) and
entropy rank ($\HR$)---that map a score correlation matrix to a single number
in $[1, M]$, where $M$ is the ensemble size and $1$ corresponds to identical
systems.

We then build four systems that differ in one architectural axis each---pooling
strategy (Pool vs.\ MHFA), cepstral representation (LFCC branch), graph topology
(AASIST-graph layer~\cite{gat_spoof}), and backbone family (WavLM vs.\
XLS-R)---while holding training corpus, augmentation policy, optimiser, and seed
constant. The measured independence is $\PR{=}1.10$ and $\HR{=}1.26$: four systems
reducing to one.

% Part D: Additional contributions.
Two additional findings complement the independence measurement. First, the
ASVspoof~5 development split cannot select among these systems: the Spearman rank
correlation between development and evaluation \eer{} over all 15 non-empty member
subsets is $\rho{=}+0.011$ ($p{=}0.97$), rising only to $+0.236$ with an added
neural-codec development view. Our protocol therefore selects a system 0.28~\eer{}
points worse than the best system in our pool, and we report both numbers and
treat the gap as a measurement of the selection set rather than a method result.
Second, adversarial robustness experiments across five defence routes show that
waveform-space adversarial training is the only route to move white-box PGD attack
success below 1.000, at a cost of $+3.78$~\eer{} points of clean accuracy---a
trade no deployed system would accept.

% Part E: Contributions list.
The contributions of this paper are as follows.
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=2pt]
\item We introduce $\PR$ and $\HR$ as model-agnostic, closed-form metrics for
  quantifying ensemble independence from a score correlation matrix.
\item We conduct the first systematic study of four architectural axes on a
  single backbone, finding that no axis except a change of temporal view
  moves the independence metric.
\item We expose a complete selection failure on the ASVspoof~5 development split,
  quantify its cost at 0.28~\eer{} points, and provide a codec-augmented
  development view that partially mitigates it.
\item We characterise adversarial robustness across five defence routes and
  report the first sub-unity white-box PGD success on this task, alongside
  an honest account of the accuracy cost.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Self-Supervised Front Ends for Anti-Spoofing}

Large-scale self-supervised pre-training has transformed anti-spoofing
countermeasures over the past four years. wav2vec~2.0~\cite{wav2vec2} trains a
convolutional waveform encoder followed by a Transformer with a contrastive
loss over quantised targets; HuBERT~\cite{hubert} replaces the contrastive loss
with offline cluster targets obtained by $k$-means on MFCC features; WavLM~\cite{wavlm}
adds a masked-speech-denoising auxiliary loss to the HuBERT objective, yielding
representations strong across both generation and discrimination tasks. XLS-R~\cite{xlsr}
scales wav2vec~2.0 pre-training to cross-lingual data at 300M and 2B parameters.
Wang and Yamagishi~\cite{wang2021ssl} provide a systematic study of these front
ends on ASVspoof~2019 and~2021 and find that fine-tuning the backbone markedly
outperforms freezing it. Tak et al.~\cite{tak2022wav2vec} show that fine-tuning
wav2vec~2.0 with RawBoost augmentation achieves then-state-of-the-art on both
ASVspoof~2021 tasks. Our work uses WavLM-Large as the primary backbone and
XLS-R-300M as a secondary backbone, adapting both with low-rank
adaptation~\cite{lora} to limit trainable parameters to 3.15M---roughly 1\% of
the backbone's 315M parameters.

\subsection{Back-End Design and Pooling}

The back-end converts per-layer, per-frame SSL representations into a binary
score. Conventional designs apply time-mean pooling across frames and a learned
weighting across layers, followed by a two-layer classifier. Attentive statistics
pooling~\cite{attentive_pool} replaces the time average with a weighted mean and
standard deviation, learning frame-level weights from a context vector. Multi-head
factorised attentive pooling (MHFA)~\cite{mhfa} decouples the layer selection
stage from the frame selection stage by learning separate key-producing and
value-producing layer weightings; the comparison then runs across multiple
attention heads aligned to phonetic cluster prototypes, originally designed for
speaker verification. Graph attention networks for anti-spoofing~\cite{gat_spoof}
model spectral sub-band relationships as a graph and attend over both frequency
and time nodes. AASIST~\cite{aasist} extends graph attention with heterogeneous
spectro-temporal cross-attention and a master node mechanism. Our Graph member
reuses only the graph attention layer of AASIST without its heterogeneous
cross-attention or master node; no result here should be cited as evidence about
the full AASIST architecture. RawNet2~\cite{rawnet2} operates end-to-end from the
raw waveform, using Sinc-based filter banks and gated recurrent units to avoid
hand-engineered spectral features.

\subsection{Data Augmentation and Codec Simulation}

RawBoost~\cite{rawboost} augments anti-spoofing data at the waveform level by
injecting impulsive, stationary coloured, or linear predictive residual noise,
targeting the low-level artefacts most sensitive to synthesis. Background noise
augmentation with MUSAN~\cite{musan} provides a complementary out-of-domain
signal. A central engineering decision in this work is applying the neural codec
EnCodec~\cite{encodec} on the GPU during the training forward pass. The evaluation
set conditions C04 and C07 are EnCodec-coded, and because EnCodec requires
GPU-based inference, standard CPU-bound DataLoaders cannot simulate these
conditions during data loading. Applying the codec online, inside the training
forward pass, was the change that turned C04 and C07 from the hardest conditions
(before) to near-average conditions (after).

\subsection{Generative Attacks and Adversarial Filters}

Neural text-to-speech and voice conversion provide the source of spoofed speech in
the ASVspoof challenges. WaveNet~\cite{wavenet} is an autoregressive waveform model
that generates samples one-step at a time from a mixture-of-logistics output
distribution; HiFi-GAN~\cite{hifigan} replaces the autoregressive decoder with a
multi-period, multi-scale discriminator setup that produces high-fidelity output
at faster-than-real-time throughput. Adversarial post-filters push these systems
further: Malafide~\cite{malafide} learns a convolutive filter on top of a
synthesiser specifically to evade a target detector, and Malacopula~\cite{malacopula}
extends this to a non-linear filter bank. These attack conditions appear in the
ASVspoof~5 evaluation set but, as Section~\ref{sec:results} shows, they are not
the hardest conditions for our systems---codec degradation is.

\subsection{Evaluation Datasets and Out-of-Domain Transfer}

The ASVspoof challenge series~\cite{asvspoof2019,asvspoof2021,liu2023asvspoof,
asvspoof5} has been the dominant anti-spoofing benchmark since 2015, providing
controlled training, development, and evaluation splits with disjoint attack sets
across partitions. WaveFake~\cite{wavefake} provides a complementary evaluation
against six neural vocoders in a more controlled setting that allows per-generator
analysis. M\"{u}ller et al.~\cite{intothewild} introduced the In-the-Wild dataset
by collecting 31{,}779 utterances from 54 public figures through online sources;
their key finding---that lab-trained detectors degrade by up to an order of
magnitude on found audio---motivates our out-of-domain reporting in
Section~\ref{sec:results}. Serrano et al.~\cite{serrano2025} study layer selection
and score-level fusion for cross-corpus generalisation and find that fusing
encoders trained on different SSL layer subsets improves transfer.

\subsection{Adversarial Robustness in Anti-Spoofing}

Projected gradient descent (PGD)~\cite{madry2018} is the standard white-box
adversarial attack: it iteratively perturbs an input by following the gradient
of the loss, projecting each step back into an $\ell_p$ ball around the original.
Applied to speech deepfakes, it asks whether an attacker who can compute gradients
through the detector can force the detector to classify a spoofed utterance as
genuine. Prior work in adversarial anti-spoofing has found SSL-based detectors
to be highly susceptible under even modest perturbation budgets. Our robustness
section contributes the first systematic comparison of five defence routes on the
same base system, including a waveform-level adversarial training baseline that
is the only one to move white-box success below 1.000.

\subsection{Ensemble Diversity Metrics}

The diversity literature in general machine learning provides several scalar
summaries of ensemble member disagreement: $Q$-statistic, disagreement measure,
double-fault measure, and $\kappa$-statistic, all defined on binary prediction
pairs~\cite{rawnet2}. These pairwise measures do not generalise cleanly to
real-valued scores or to sets larger than two, and they confound disagreement
with accuracy. Principal-component analysis of score correlation matrices has
been used informally in the speaker recognition and anti-spoofing communities
to visualise system redundancy, but participation ratio and entropy rank---defined
precisely in Section~\ref{sec:rank}---provide the single-number summary that
pairwise measures lack. To our knowledge, no prior anti-spoofing paper has applied
these metrics to characterise an ensemble before combining it.

Table~\ref{tab:data} and Fig.~\ref{fig:dataset} summarise the corpora. Three
structural properties of ASVspoof~5~\cite{asvspoof5} are consequential for every
finding in this paper.

\textbf{Disjoint attacks across partitions.} A01--A08, A09--A16, and A17--A32 do
not overlap. A system is always evaluated on spoofing algorithms it has never seen
in training, preventing any form of attack memorisation. In our preliminary runs
this design repeatedly penalised models with overfitted score distributions.

\textbf{Channel mismatch between selection and test.} Train and development are
100\% uncoded; the evaluation set is 75\% coded with eleven real codec conditions
(C01--C11). This means the split used to select models contains none of the
conditions that dominate evaluation difficulty. We simulate ten of the eleven
codec conditions during training by applying codecs on the CPU side (C01--C03,
C05--C06, C08--C11 via \texttt{torchaudio}) and EnCodec~\cite{encodec}
on the GPU side (C04, C07). Only C11 (physical device variation) is not simulated.
Section~\ref{sec:selection} shows this mismatch is severe enough to make
development-set ranking indistinguishable from chance.

\textbf{Source-utterance structure inflates the apparent sample size.}
Every coded clip in the evaluation set carries a \texttt{CODEC\_SEED} identifier
pointing at its original utterance, and all 509{,}172 coded clips have their
source also present as a clean clip. The 680{,}774 evaluation clips therefore
derive from only \textbf{171{,}602 distinct source utterances} (mean 3.97 clips
per source). A bootstrap procedure that resamples clips as if they were independent
observations underestimates the standard error of any metric by approximately
$\sqrt{4}$, producing confidence intervals roughly $2\times$ too narrow. All
intervals reported in this paper use a clustered bootstrap that resamples source
utterances, not clips.

In-the-Wild~\cite{intothewild} is used exclusively for out-of-domain reporting. Its
inverted class prior (62.8\% bona fide vs.\ 20.4\% in ASVspoof~5 eval) makes
\mindcf{} under the 0.05 spoof prior incomparable with the ASVspoof numbers; we
report \eer{} only for this set.

\subsection{Common Pipeline}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering

\label{fig:arch}

Fig.~\ref{fig:arch} shows the pipeline. A waveform $\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^T$ is
first augmented---one of eight codec conditions with probability 0.5, RawBoost
noise~\cite{rawboost} with probability 0.4, MUSAN~\cite{musan} additive noise with
probability 0.3, and EnCodec~\cite{encodec} on the GPU with probability 0.3---then
passed through WavLM-Large~\cite{wavlm} adapted with LoRA of rank~16 applied to
every query and value projection~\cite{lora}. This yields $L{=}25$ layer
activations $\{\mathbf{H}^l\}_{l=1}^{L}$, each of shape $T'\times 1024$. The
backbone has 315M parameters; LoRA adds 3.15M trainable parameters (1.0\%).
Members are combined by the unweighted mean of scores standardised on the
speaker-disjoint \texttt{calib} split; the combiner has no parameters fit on
evaluation data.

\subsection{Back-End Members}

\textit{Pool} (0.26M parameters). A scalar softmax weight $w_l$ is learned for
each of the $L$ layers. The pooled representation is $\mathbf{h}{=}\sum_l
w_l\,\overline{\mathbf{H}}^l$ where $\overline{\mathbf{H}}^l{=}T'^{-1}\sum_t
\mathbf{H}^l_t$ is the frame mean. A two-layer MLP maps $\mathbf{h}$ to a score.
Pool is the conventional baseline design; it discards all temporal structure.

\textit{MHFA} (0.53M). Following Peng et al.~\cite{mhfa}, two separate softmax
weightings $\{w_l^K\}$ and $\{w_l^V\}$ produce a key representation
$\mathbf{K}{=}\sum_l w_l^K \mathbf{H}^l$ and a value representation
$\mathbf{V}{=}\sum_l w_l^V \mathbf{H}^l$ (both retaining the time axis). A
multi-head scaled-dot-product attention over $T'$ time steps then pools from
$\mathbf{V}$ using $\mathbf{K}$ as the query sequence. MHFA is the only member
that retains frame-level temporal structure throughout the back-end.

\textit{LFCC} (0.50M). Adds a second \emph{representation} branch: 20
linear-frequency cepstral coefficients with $\Delta$ and $\Delta^2$ features,
computed frame-synchronously with the backbone output and concatenated before
attentive pooling~\cite{attentive_pool}. The cepstral features operate on the
raw waveform and carry information about spectral envelope shape that the SSL
backbone does not explicitly encode.

\textit{Graph} (0.30M). Adds a second \emph{topology}: graph attention is applied
over a bipartite graph whose nodes are time slots and backbone layers, reusing the
graph attention layer implementation from AASIST~\cite{aasist} but without its
heterogeneous cross-attention or master-node aggregation. The graph topology gives
each output node access to all time slots of any layer, unlike Pool's strict
per-layer averaging.

\section{Experimental Protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}

Models are selected on the development \texttt{select} split (attacks A09--A16,
uncoded) after training; score-normalisation statistics are fit on the
speaker-disjoint \texttt{calib} half of the same split; the evaluation set is
scored exactly once. Metrics are \eer{}, \mindcf{} under the official ASVspoof~5
cost model (spoof prior 0.05, $C_\text{miss}{=}1$, $C_\text{fa}{=}10$)~\cite{tdcf},
and $\text{min}C_\text{llr}$. Paired differences are tested with a clustered
bootstrap (5{,}000 resamples) over source utterances.

All systems use seed 1337, batch size 32, eight epochs, head learning rate
$10^{-3}$ and backbone learning rate $10^{-5}$ under AdamW with weight decay
$10^{-4}$. The learning rate is held constant throughout training; no scheduling
or early stopping is applied. Checkpoints are saved after each epoch and the best
checkpoint by development \eer{} is used for evaluation. \textbf{Single-seed
limitation (stated up front):} earlier three-seed experiments on this codebase
gave a 0.33~\eer{} point spread on frozen single models, which is larger than
the 0.10-point fusion gain we report. Seed variance for the systems in
Table~\ref{tab:members} is therefore not established, and multi-seed replication
of the fusion gain is left as future work.

Score normalisation uses additive score standardisation fit on the \texttt{calib}
split: for each system $k$, $s'_k(x) = (s_k(x) - \mu_k)/\sigma_k$ where $\mu_k$
and $\sigma_k$ are the mean and standard deviation of $s_k$ over the genuine
utterances in \texttt{calib}. Fusion scores are the unweighted mean of normalised
scores. This calibration ensures that the fusion denominator does not implicitly
up-weight systems with higher raw-score variance, which would bias the effective
rank measurement.

\subsection{Ensemble Independence}
\label{sec:rank}

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/fig6_spectrum}
\caption{(a)~Pearson score correlations on the evaluation set. (b)~Eigenspectrum
of the correlation matrix: PC1 explains 95.5\% of the variance. (c)~PC1 loadings
are within 0.006 of 0.50 on every member---the leading component is the
unweighted mean, not a differential signal.}
\label{fig:spectrum}
\end{figure*}

We characterise the ensemble by the eigenspectrum of the score correlation matrix
$\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times M}$ (normalised scores, $M{=}4$ members).
Two scalar summaries are used:
\begin{align}
\PR(\mathbf{R}) &= \frac{\bigl(\sum_i \lambda_i\bigr)^2}{\sum_i \lambda_i^2},
\label{eq:pr}\\
\HR(\mathbf{R}) &= \exp\!\left(-\sum_i p_i \log p_i\right),\quad
  p_i = \frac{\lambda_i}{\sum_j \lambda_j},
\label{eq:hr}
\end{align}
where $\lambda_i$ are the eigenvalues of $\mathbf{R}$. Both metrics equal 1 for
identical members and $M$ for perfectly orthogonal members.

Fig.~\ref{fig:spectrum} gives the answer: $\PR{=}1.10$, $\HR{=}1.26$, with PC1
explaining 95.5\% of the variance and PC1 loadings $0.501, 0.496, 0.501, 0.502$.
These loadings are within 0.006 of $1/\sqrt{M}{=}0.50$: the leading component
is, within numerical precision, the unweighted mean score---it carries no
differential information. On the out-of-domain set the independence is $\PR{=}1.15$,
$\HR{=}1.30$ of 4. Changing pooling, representation, topology \emph{and} backbone
together shifts the effective rank by only 0.1. The best two-member fusion
improves on its best member by 0.18~\eer{} points; adding a third or fourth member
adds nothing. A third member adds zero gain because the pool has no independent
signal left to contribute beyond the variance reduction already achieved by two.

We use PGD~\cite{madry2018} under an SNR-constrained $L_2$ budget (30\,dB),
with white-box access (gradients through the full model), transfer access

(ii)~a four-member score-averaging ensemble, (iii)~white-box on WavLM evaluated

wrong threat surface). SPSA black-box at 320 queries succeeds on approximately
1\% of clips for all routes, confirming that query-limited black-box attackers
remain manageable.

\textbf{Waveform adversarial training is the one route that moves.} Replacing
half of each training batch with 30\,dB PGD examples crafted through the backbone
drops white-box success from 1.000 to 0.953 and transfer success from 1.000 to
0.739---the first sub-unity numbers in this setting. The matched control system
(trained identically but without the adversarial half) sits at 1.000 on every row,
confirming the reduction is the defence rather than the modified training schedule.

\textbf{The price is severe.} Clean evaluation \eer{} rises from 3.31\% to 7.09\%,
a paired difference of $+0.0378$ (95\% CI $[+0.0372,\,+0.0384]$ over 680{,}774
clips clustered on source utterances). Codec conditions take the worst damage
(C07: 11.06\%, C08: 10.08\%), and the feature-space attempt---the mismatch control
---reached 13.95\% before waveform training corrected the threat surface and
recovered 6.9~\eer{} points of clean accuracy. A 4.7-point reduction in white-box
success in exchange for 3.78~\eer{} points of clean accuracy is a trade no deployed
system would accept.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Why Architectural Variation Does Not Produce Independence}

The $\PR{=}1.10$ finding contradicts the intuition that more architecturally
distinct systems should disagree more on errors. The reason is structural, not
accidental. Every member ingests the same backbone features, and those features
are dominated by a speech identity signal that is shared across all layers and
compression schemes. Varying \emph{how} those features are aggregated---by pooling
strategy, topology, or the addition of a second branch---changes the decision
boundary but does not change the evidence space the boundary is drawn over. Since
all members draw on the same evidence, their per-utterance decisions are correlated
at the evidence level, regardless of architectural differences.

This reasoning predicts the one exception: MHFA, which retains the time axis
rather than averaging it, gains access to temporal structure that every other
member discards. A vocoder artefact that manifests at a single frame or a short
temporal segment is visible to MHFA but invisible to Pool after the mean. This
is why MHFA is the only member with $\rho_{\text{Pool}}{<}0.95$, and why the
Pool$+$MHFA fusion is the only two-member pair that delivers a measurable gain.
The conclusion for practitioners is concrete: \emph{to buy independence in a
shared-backbone ensemble, change which information axes the model can access, not
how the model processes the features it already has}.

\subsection{Implications for Ensemble Design}

The participation ratio formulation~\eqref{eq:pr} and entropy rank~\eqref{eq:hr}
provide actionable criteria. Before investing in training additional members, one
can score a candidate pool on a held-out set, compute $\mathbf{R}$, and check
whether adding a new member increases $\PR$ by more than a threshold (we suggest
0.1 per added member as a rough criterion based on our data). A member that does
not move $\PR$ can be discarded without training the full ensemble.

The selection failure result has a complementary practical implication: benchmark
rank on a development set without coded conditions is not a reliable proxy for
evaluation performance on a coded set. The 0.28-point gap between our selected
system and the oracle is not a large absolute difference, but it demonstrates
that any ablation study or architecture search reported only on the uncoded
development split of ASVspoof~5 may produce misleading conclusions.

\subsection{The Role of the Backbone in Error Correlation}

A natural question is whether changing the backbone entirely---rather than
the back-end---would increase independence. Our fifth member, XLS-R-300M~\cite{xlsr},
provides a partial answer. A Pool back-end trained on XLS-R reaches 5.12\%~\eer{}
on the evaluation set, and its score correlation with the WavLM-Pool member is
0.934: somewhat lower than the within-backbone correlation of 0.965 (LFCC) but
still far from independent. This suggests that different backbones trained on the
same task with the same loss learn correlated spoofness indicators at the feature
level, even when their pre-training objectives and architectures differ. The
implication for multi-backbone ensembles is sobering: backbone diversity alone
does not guarantee error independence, and practitioners should measure $\PR$ for
any combination before investing in the remaining training runs.

\subsection{On Adversarial Robustness as a Deployment Criterion}

The robustness findings are deliberately framed as measurements rather than
improvements. The field frequently reports that a proposed defence ``improves
robustness'' without simultaneously reporting the accuracy cost at comparable
operating conditions, which makes comparisons across papers difficult to
interpret. We report both numbers explicitly: the only defence that moves the
white-box frontier also moves the clean \eer{} by $+3.78$ points, a $2.1\times$
degradation in ordinary accuracy. Whether this trade is acceptable depends on
the deployment threat model; for most applications, a detector that correctly
classifies 93\% of spoofed utterances as genuine under attack while degrading
substantially in the clean case is not usable. We treat this as a measurement
of the frontier, not a claim that the frontier cannot be pushed further.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limit}

\textbf{Single seed.} Every system in Table~\ref{tab:members} uses seed 1337.
Earlier three-seed experiments on frozen single models gave a 0.33~\eer{} point
spread, larger than the 0.10-point fusion gain we report. The fusion gain is not
established against seed variance; multi-seed replication is the clearest open risk
for a follow-up.

\textbf{One training corpus.} We train on ASVspoof~5 alone. USTC-KXDIGIT~\cite{ustc2024}
trains on four corpora including WaveFake~\cite{wavefake} and internal data. We
cannot separate the effect of a weaker architecture from narrower supervision.

\textbf{Clustered bootstrap required.} Clip-level bootstrap intervals on this
corpus are approximately $2\times$ too narrow due to the source-utterance
structure. Any prior or subsequent paper that reports clip-level intervals on
ASVspoof~5 may have overconfident claims; our clustered intervals survive
the correction because the differences are many standard errors wide.

\textbf{One out-of-domain set.} In-the-Wild covers 54 public figures and internet
audio with an inverted class prior. One out-of-domain set establishes that a
gap exists; it does not characterise generalisation to arbitrary deployment
conditions.

\textbf{The Graph member is not AASIST.} Results from the Graph member should not
be cited as evidence about the performance of the full AASIST architecture~\cite{aasist}.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper asked whether architectural variation is sufficient to produce
independent errors in a shared-backbone ensemble of speech deepfake detectors.
The answer is no: four detectors differing in pooling, representation, topology,
and backbone amount to $\PR{=}1.10$ independent systems out of four. The leading
principal component of the score correlation matrix explains 95.5\% of the
variance and is, within numerical precision, the unweighted mean score. The only
exception was the member that changed \emph{what it could see}---frame-level
attention over the time axis rather than mean pooling---not how it processed
features it already had.

Three practical findings surround this measurement. First, the ASVspoof~5
development split cannot rank these systems ($\rho{=}+0.011$); the information
needed to discriminate them---codec conditions---is absent from the development
set by design. Adding a codec-augmented development view improves ordering but
does not reach statistical reliability. Second, the best in-domain member
transfers worst out of domain, so optimising for leaderboard position on
ASVspoof~5 risks selecting the system least suitable for deployment. Third, the
only adversarial defence that moves the white-box attack frontier imposes a
$2.1\times$ accuracy penalty, and we report this plainly so that the community
can make informed comparisons.

Our selected system, Pool$+$MHFA, achieves \textbf{3.21\%~\eer{} / 0.0905~\mindcf{}},
outperforming the best published single system (SZU-AFS at 4.04\%~\cite{szuafs}).
The oracle subset reaches 2.93\%~\eer{}, and the 0.28-point gap between them
is our best estimate of the selection signal's cost on this benchmark. The more
lasting contribution is the methodology: participation ratio and entropy rank
provide a closed-form, model-agnostic criterion for measuring how many independent
systems an ensemble truly contains, applicable to any detection task where
per-instance scores are available.
